# Summative Assessment: Make Your Case

## Purpose

This assessment measures whether you can build, support, and defend an argument on a debatable topic. It is the final assessment for the debate skills lesson.

## Learning objectives

By the end of the lesson, you will be able to:

1. **Construct a claim** that takes a clear position on a debatable resolution.
2. **Support the claim** with reasoning and credible evidence (the Claim–Evidence–Reasoning structure).
3. **Anticipate and rebut** at least one counterargument.
4. **Communicate the argument** in an organized way for a specific audience.

## Choose how you show your understanding

Pick **one** format. All formats are graded with the same rubric.

| Format | What you create | Requirements |
|---|---|---|
| **A. Recorded speech** | A video or audio recording of a constructive speech | 3–5 minutes, with captions or a transcript |
| **B. Written case** | A written argument (essay or debate brief) | 600–900 words |
| **C. Visual argument** | An annotated slide deck, infographic, or argument map | 6–10 slides or one detailed map, with speaker notes or written explanation for every part |
| **D. Live debate** | A short one-on-one debate with a classmate or the teacher | 3-minute constructive + 2-minute rebuttal |

Have a different idea? Email your teacher with a short proposal before you start. Any format is approved as long as it lets you show all four learning objectives.

## Instructions

1. **Choose a resolution** from the list your teacher provides, or propose your own debatable resolution.
2. **Choose your side** (for or against) and write a one-sentence claim.
3. **Research** and choose at least **two credible sources**. Record each source's author, date, and why it is credible.
4. **Build your case** using Claim–Evidence–Reasoning for at least two supporting points.
5. **Identify the strongest counterargument** to your claim and write a rebuttal to it.
6. **Create your final product** in the format you chose, following the requirements in the table above.
7. **Check your work** with the self-check checklist below.
8. **Submit** your work (and a list of your sources) to your teacher by October 10, 2026.

## Alignment

|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Construct a claim | Learn, Part 1: Write a claim | Step 2 | Claim |
| 2. Support the claim | Learn, Part 2: Evidence and reasoning (includes judging sources) | Steps 3–4 | Evidence and reasoning |

## Rubric (20 points)

| Criterion | 5 – Exceeds | 4 – Meets | 3 – Approaching | 1–2 – Beginning |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Claim** | The claim is clear, specific, and takes a firm position on the resolution. It is restated effectively in the conclusion. | The claim is clear and takes a position on the resolution. | The claim takes a position but is vague or only loosely connected to the resolution. | The claim is missing, or does not take a position. |
| **Evidence and reasoning** | Two or more credible, cited sources support each point, and the reasoning explains exactly how the evidence proves the claim. | Two credible, cited sources support the case, and reasoning connects evidence to the claim. | Evidence is present, but a source is not credible or not cited, or the reasoning is unclear. | Little or no evidence, or no explanation of how it supports the claim. |
| **Counterargument and rebuttal** | The strongest counterargument is stated fairly and rebutted with evidence and reasoning. | A relevant counterargument is stated and rebutted with reasoning. | A counterargument is stated, but the rebuttal is weak or missing. | No counterargument is addressed. |
| **Organization and delivery** | The argument flows logically with clear transitions, and is delivered (spoken, written, or visual) in a way that fits the audience. | The argument has a clear introduction, body, and conclusion, and is easy to follow. | The structure is present but hard to follow in places. | The argument is disorganized or hard to follow. |

Delivery is judged by what fits your format. For example, a recorded speech is judged on pace and clarity, a written case on paragraphing and transitions, and a visual argument on layout and labeling. It is never judged on accent, speech patterns, or handwriting.

## Self-check before you submit

- [ ] My claim takes a clear position on the resolution.
- [ ] I have at least two supporting points, each with evidence and reasoning.
- [ ] I cited at least two credible sources.
- [ ] I addressed the strongest counterargument and rebutted it.
- [ ] My work has a clear beginning, middle, and end.
- [ ] My work meets the length or time requirement for my format.
- [ ] If I used video or audio, I included captions or a transcript. If I used images, I included alt text.

## Work sample

An annotated example of a "Meets" written case is below. Your teacher will also share examples of the other formats.

> **Resolution:** Schools should start no earlier than 8:30 a.m.
>
> **Claim:** *(Claim)* Schools should start at 8:30 a.m. or later because later start times improve student health and learning.
>
> **Point 1:** *(Evidence)* The American Academy of Pediatrics recommends that middle and high schools start at 8:30 a.m. or later so students can get enough sleep. *(Reasoning)* Teenagers' sleep cycles shift later during puberty, so early starts cut into sleep they cannot make up by going to bed earlier.
>
> **Counterargument and rebuttal:** *(Counterargument)* Opponents argue later start times make scheduling after-school activities and bus routes harder. *(Rebuttal)* However, these are logistical problems that districts can solve, while lost sleep directly affects every student's health each day.
