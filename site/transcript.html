Audio Walkthrough Transcript

This is the full text of the audio walkthrough on the Learn the skills page. Read it instead of listening, or follow along while you listen.

Introduction

Hi, and welcome to Make Your Case. Have you ever been in an argument where you knew you were right, but you just couldn't convince the other person? Today you'll learn four skills that fix that: writing a claim, backing it up with evidence and reasoning, answering the other side, and presenting it all clearly. Let's go.

Part 1: Write a claim

Every debate starts with a resolution. That's a statement people can reasonably disagree about, like "Schools should ban cell phones during class time." Your claim is the side you take, in one clear sentence.

A strong claim does three things. It picks a side. It's specific. And someone could actually argue against it. "Phones are kind of distracting sometimes" doesn't pick a side. "Schools should ban cell phones during class time because phones distract students from learning" does.

Pause here and try it: write one claim about a topic you care about.

Part 2: Evidence and reasoning

A claim by itself is just an opinion. To prove it, we use Claim, Evidence, Reasoning, or C-E-R. Picture a building. Evidence is the foundation at the bottom. Reasoning is the middle floor that connects it. And your claim sits on top.

Evidence is facts, data, or expert statements, and you always say where it came from. Reasoning explains how the evidence proves your claim. Here's a trick: after your evidence, say "This matters because…" and finish the sentence. That's your reasoning.

Before you use a source, ask: Who wrote it? When? Why? And can I confirm it somewhere else?

Part 3: Answer the other side

The strongest arguments deal with the other side head-on. A counterargument is the best point your opponent could make. Your rebuttal answers it, in four steps: They say. But. Because. Therefore.

For example: They say students need phones for emergencies. But a class-time ban doesn't take that away. Because students keep their phones nearby and can use the office phone. Therefore, families can still reach them, and class stays distraction-free.

Part 4: Present your argument, with a sample speech

Organize your argument in five parts: hook and claim, point one, point two, rebuttal, and conclusion. Use signposts like "My first point is" so your audience can follow. Here's a short sample speech:

Picture trying to learn fractions while your phone buzzes every few minutes. Schools should ban cell phones during class time because phones distract students from learning.

My first point is that phones pull attention away from lessons. In a Pew Research Center survey published in 2024, seventy-two percent of high school teachers said phone distraction is a major problem in their classroom. When phones are put away, that distraction is gone.

My second point is that experts recommend limiting phones in school. UNESCO's 2023 report advises using technology in school only when it supports learning. A class-time ban does exactly that.

The other side might say students need phones for emergencies. But a class-time ban still lets students keep phones nearby and use the office phone, so families can still reach them.

In conclusion, banning phones during class protects students' time to learn while keeping them safe. Thank you.

Now it's your turn. Head to the Make Your Case assessment and pick the way you want to show what you learned. Good luck!
